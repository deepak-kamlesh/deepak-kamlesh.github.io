My central research interest is the surprising connection between the arithmetic of **periods**—numbers and functions that arise naturally in geometry and number theory—and the mathematics used to describe **particle interactions**. The possibility that these quantities, arising from seemingly distant subjects, obey common algebraic structures and symmetries is one of the principal motivations for my work.

## What are periods?

Many familiar numbers can be expressed as integrals of algebraic functions over geometrically defined domains. Such numbers are called **periods**. Examples include **π**, which is related to the geometry of a circle; **log 2**, expressible as the integral of 1/x from 1 to 2; and special values of the **Riemann zeta function**, such as ζ(2) and ζ(3).

The subject extends well beyond these classical examples. Periods also arise from **elliptic curves**, algebraic varieties and their cohomology. Functions such as the **beta and hypergeometric functions**, elliptic integrals and multiple polylogarithms provide rich families of period integrals (for appropriate parameters and specializations). Many of these same functions appear throughout geometry, number theory and mathematical physics.

One fundamental problem is to understand the algebraic relations among periods: why do two apparently different integrals sometimes give the same number, or satisfy unexpected identities? The theory of **motives** provides a conceptual language for organizing such questions.

## What are scattering amplitudes?

In quantum field theory, a **scattering amplitude** is a mathematical quantity describing the transition from an initial configuration of particles to a final one. Amplitudes are not themselves probabilities: they are complex quantities whose magnitudes and interference determine the probabilities and cross sections that experiments can measure.

Physicists often calculate amplitudes perturbatively, as an expansion in an interaction strength. The terms in this expansion are represented by **Feynman diagrams** and evaluated using integrals. Surprisingly, many of these integrals, or coefficients in their expansions, are periods or belong to families of special functions familiar from number theory and algebraic geometry. Multiple zeta values, polylogarithms and elliptic integrals are prominent examples; more complicated geometries lead to richer classes of functions.

## Why motivic Galois theory?

Classical Galois theory explains symmetries among the roots of polynomial equations. **Motivic Galois theory** seeks an analogous understanding of the algebraic structures and symmetries underlying periods. Its tools, including the **motivic coaction**, can reveal relations and organizing principles that are not obvious from a numerical value or an integral representation alone.

The exciting point is that these arithmetic structures also appear in the calculation of scattering amplitudes. They can expose relationships between different functions and calculations, constrain possible answers, and suggest more systematic ways to organize perturbative results. Why the mathematical symmetries of periods should be so closely connected to the laws describing particle interactions—and how far this connection extends, including beyond perturbation theory—is a central question motivating my research.

For an accessible account of the appearance of periods in particle physics, see Kevin Hartnett's article in *Quanta Magazine*: [“Strange Numbers Found in Particle Collisions”](https://www.quantamagazine.org/strange-numbers-found-in-particle-collisions-20161115/).
