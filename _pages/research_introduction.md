One of the central motivations for my research is a surprising connection between **pure mathematics and the behaviour of elementary particles**. Many of the numbers appearing in calculations of particle interactions are *periods*—algebraic integrals studied in arithmetic geometry and number theory—and the functions involved often arise from families of such integrals. As a result, **the algebraic structure and symmetries of periods can constrain the possible forms of physical predictions**. I am interested in understanding **how and why this happens**, and whether related structures can illuminate observables in other areas of physics.

## What are periods?

In mathematics, **periods** are numbers that can be expressed as convergent integrals of algebraic differential forms over domains defined by algebraic equations and inequalities. **Algebraic numbers** themselves are examples of periods, but the class is much richer: it includes familiar constants such as **π** and **log 2**, special values of the **Riemann zeta function** such as **ζ(2)** and **ζ(3)**, and **multiple zeta values**, which generalise ordinary zeta values and satisfy intricate algebraic relations.

Periods also arise from the geometry of **elliptic curves** and more general algebraic varieties. When such integrals vary with parameters, they give rise to important families of special functions, including **logarithms, polylogarithms, elliptic integrals, beta functions and hypergeometric functions**.

These numbers and functions possess rich algebraic structures and symmetries. **Classical Galois theory** studies algebraic numbers through the symmetries of their conjugates. **Motivic Galois theory** seeks to extend this perspective to periods, providing a framework for investigating their more general symmetries and the algebraic relations among them.

## What are scattering amplitudes?

Elementary particles interact according to the laws of quantum field theory. For example, an electron and a positron can collide and produce other particles. A **[scattering amplitude](https://scattering-amplitudes.mpp.mpg.de/welcome)** is a mathematical quantity describing such an interaction. The probabilities of different outcomes are calculated from amplitudes, including interference between different possibilities.

Physicists often compute amplitudes as expansions in the strength of interactions. The successive contributions are represented by **Feynman diagrams** and evaluated using integrals. Many of these integrals, and the functions describing their dependence on particle energies and momenta, are periods or parameter-dependent relatives of periods.

These connections occur in familiar physical settings, not only in abstract theories. For instance, **multiple zeta values and polylogarithms** appear in higher-order calculations relevant to particle collisions. The algebraic structure of polylogarithms has been used to simplify expressions for **Higgs-boson amplitudes**. Beyond scattering processes, related period structures arise in the **electron's anomalous magnetic moment** (often called *g − 2*), a precisely measured quantum correction to the electron's magnetic properties. Researchers have studied the **Galois coaction** on polylogarithmic contributions to this quantity. 

String theory supplies further examples: **beta and hypergeometric functions** occur in tree-level string amplitudes, while **multiple zeta values, elliptic multiple zeta values and modular graph forms** feature in low-energy expansions and at higher orders in string perturbation theory. More complicated quantum-field-theory integrals can likewise involve **elliptic periods and elliptic polylogarithms**.

The relevance of periods extends beyond particle physics and string theory. In **gravitational physics**, calculations of gravitational-wave scattering can involve **elliptic iterated integrals**. In **cosmology**, certain integrals used to study correlations of primordial fluctuations admit descriptions as **twisted periods**.

## Why is this connection remarkable?

The striking point is not merely that mathematics and physics use similar techniques. **It is that algebraic structures discovered in the study of periods can constrain calculations of how fundamental particles interact.** Once a physical observable is expressed through periods or period functions, the identities and symmetries of those mathematical objects become relevant to the observable itself.

Tools such as the **motivic coaction** can uncover relationships among periods, organise complicated calculations and—where the relevant structures and principles apply—restrict the combinations of numbers and functions that can occur in physical observables.

These mathematical constraints do not replace the physical laws that determine particle interactions. Rather, they reveal additional structure in the quantities those laws predict. **Why should the arithmetic symmetries of periods exert this kind of influence on physical calculations, and how far does that influence extend?** Understanding this connection, and developing the mathematical structures that explain it, is a central motivation for my research.

For an accessible account of this connection, see Kevin Hartnett's [*Strange Numbers Found in Particle Collisions*](https://www.quantamagazine.org/strange-numbers-found-in-particle-collisions-20161115/) in *Quanta Magazine*.
